# Final Thoughts — The WGU M.S. in Computer Science (HCI)

**Wrap-up** · Apr 1 – Aug 5, 2026 · 10 courses · 126 days

**Time:** ~240 hours on WGU work · ~155 hours of outside courses · plus 100+ hours on pwn.college and Python practice on my own

## In one line

The degree is a fast, broad survey of computer science. It's worth it if you bring your own discipline and go deeper on your own time.

## My overall verdict

I am glad that this program showed me a little bit of everything. It is a direction or a roadmap, not necessarily a comprehensive study. I also enjoyed that the program was contrite and directed, so that I can get in, get educated, and get out. It leaves me more time for other endeavors such as going deeper into topics that interest me or will lead into a career. 

My verdict is that education, certificates, and experience are the trifecta you need to get hired and get competitive, and this is enough education to check that box. I have gone to PSU and UA (Alaska) in the past and this was comparable to PSU in how it handled and covered material and knowledge transfer.

The program gave me breadth. I touched language theory, architecture, algorithms, GRC, Linux, machine learning, HCI, mobile design, software architecture, and learning technology. No single course went deep. The depth came from what I did around it: DataCamp, pwn.college, Udemy, and going straight to primary sources like NIST.

The tasks are papers and small projects, not production software. You can pass most courses faster than I did. I chose to over-learn in a few of them, especially D800, because I wanted the skills, not just the grade. You can also take a lot longer to finish this. I was putting in 12-16 hour days 6-7 days a week with few breaks. This was a sprint. And I continue to solidify what I gained from this course with review and supplemental education and projects. 

What I'd tell anyone starting: the degree is the frame. You build the house.

## Why I chose HCI over Computing Systems and AI

Human Computer Interaction is the deeper side of UX and design. It has elements of actual computer science as well as testing and research. I felt this was a bridge between the artistic side of UX and the scientific side of Computer Science. 

I wanted a masters and of the three options, this was the most interesting to me. I plan on working with HCI and UX and think that there is opportunity there with not just the UX and research side of it, but with how we now interact with computers in an age where AI/SI does a lot of the heavy lifting. Additionally, with robotics coming soon as a major front in tech, how we interact with those robots is deeply rooted in HCI. 

Usability and security are my other interest. I initially thought this would lead there, but diving further into cybersecurity has led me closer to traditional networking and cloud than HCI. Given the choice again, I would stay with this track over the others. 

## What I learned

Across ten courses, these are the lessons that stuck:

- **Scope the decision to the size of the problem.** I spent hours choosing a language for 100 lines of code.
- **A near-perfect result is a warning sign.** My 95% model was rebuilding its own answer. Always test against the simplest possible guess.
- **Report what you actually measure.** "No real difference" is a valid result when you can explain why.
- **Go to the source.** NIST documents, official docs, and the original papers beat secondhand summaries.
- **Write less.** Many of my papers ran 2–5× longer than they needed to be.
- **Outside courses often beat the textbooks.** DataCamp, pwn.college, and a few great Udemy courses taught me more than most of the assigned books.
- **Pace matters.** I burned out twice. A day off made the next week better.
- **AI is a tool, not a crutch.** I used it throughout and disclosed it. I still had to understand every line, and later I used it to find my own mistakes.

## How this program helps a career in tech

**What it does:**
- It clears degree filters. Many roles, and most government and defense roles, list a degree, and some prefer a master's.
- It gives you the vocabulary. After this degree I can follow a conversation about architecture, ML, GRC, or UX and ask good questions.
- It proves you can finish. Ten courses in four months while working is evidence of discipline.

**What it doesn't do:**
- It doesn't replace experience. The tasks are papers and small projects, not production systems.
- It doesn't make you an expert in anything. It's a survey. You pick what to go deep on.
- It won't get you hired by itself. Projects, certifications, and being able to explain your work do that.

## Advice if you're starting the program

1. **Learn Python first.** Most of the program needs it.
2. **Check your student portal on day one.** WGU includes Udemy, LinkedIn Learning, DataCamp, and Pluralsight paid accounts.
3. **Read the rubric line by line before you submit.** Especially the first few courses. These courses pass very strictly following the rubric.
4. **Write to the length asked.** Answer the rubric, then stop.
5. **Skip the weak outside courses.** My course pages say which ones were worth it.
6. **Keep your work.** Save your code, papers, and test files. I lost some of mine.
7. **Keep a study journal.** It's how I know these numbers, and it's how I remembered what I did.
8. **Competency Gets you hired** Don't cram and forget. The degree is a foot in the door. Your knowledge, skills and abilities get you hired.

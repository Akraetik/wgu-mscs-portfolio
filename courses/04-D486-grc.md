# D486 — Governance, Risk and Compliance

**Course 4 of 10** · Apr 29 – May 7, 2026 · 1 performance task · 3 textbooks

**Time:** 9 calendar days · ~15 hours on WGU work · ~8 hours of outside courses 

**How it went:** Five days in, I noticed I was overworking and took a full day off. After that I slowed down and studied the material more carefully. The paper took two sessions.

This course was very isolated from the others. No coding. It was a test of your ability to read documentation and GovTech standards like NIST. It came to me naturally since it's written similar to military documentation I've lived in for over a decade as a Non-Commissioned Officer in the US Army.

## In one line

Acting as CISO for a fictional federally funded medical center, I turned a third-party security assessment into a remediation plan mapped to NIST SP 800-53 controls. I also wrote a PCI-DSS policy for a new point-of-sale system.

## What the course covers

Authorizing and maintaining information systems with risk management frameworks. It covers compliance and regulation, data classification, security and privacy controls, audits and remediation, and risk management plans.

## What I did

- **Summarized seven security gaps** from a Security Assessment Report (SAR): missing endpoint protection, no MFA, an outdated System Security Plan, no access-control policy, insecure portal logins, PCI-DSS gaps, and missing risk and asset documentation.
- **Rated and justified five NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 controls:**
  - **AC-6 Least Privilege** — High
  - **CA-5 Plan of Action and Milestones** — Moderate
  - **CA-7 Continuous Monitoring** — High
  - **RA-3 Risk Assessment** — High
  - **RA-7 Risk Response** — Moderate
- **Wrote a remediation plan for each control:** the assets involved, the actions required (account audits, RBAC, SIEM deployment, an SP 800-30 risk assessment, a POA&M template), and the policy changes.
- **Tied each fix to the law and frameworks:** FISMA, OMB Circular A-130, the Privacy Act of 1974, and the NIST Risk Management Framework.
- **Wrote a PCI-DSS policy** for the new POS system, covering network security controls such as firewalls (Req. 1), secure configurations with no vendor default passwords (Req. 2), and anti-malware (Req. 5).

## Skills used

`NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5` `NIST RMF` `NIST SP 800-30` `PCI-DSS v4.0` `FISMA` `SSP` `SAR` `POA&M` `risk rating` `policy writing`

## What I'd do differently

- **Fifteen pages was too long.** A CISO briefing should fit on one page with a risk table. I'd lead with that.
- **I'd score risk with a matrix.** Likelihood × impact per SP 800-30, so "High" has a number behind it.
- **I'd include a sample POA&M row and a sample SIEM alert rule.** Concrete artifacts beat prose in an interview.

## Key ideas worth knowing

- The NIST RMF 7 steps: **Prepare → Categorize → Select → Implement → Assess → Authorize → Monitor**
- FIPS 199 impact levels (Low / Moderate / High)
- SP 800-53 control families (AC, AU, CA, CM, IA, IR, RA, SC, SI, and more)
- NIST CSF 2.0 functions: **Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover**
- BIA (Business Impact Analysis), BCP (Business Continuity Planning), gap analysis
- Risk responses: accept, avoid, mitigate, transfer

## Books and reading

The course ran on three textbooks. WGU students get them free as e-books through the course pages.

### *Fundamentals of Information Systems Security* (4th ed.)
**David Kim, Michael G. Solomon** · 2021 · Jones & Bartlett Learning

**What's in it:** the seven domains of a typical IT infrastructure, risks and threats, access controls, security operations, cryptography, networks, malware, and U.S. compliance laws.

**Thoughts:** I only skimmed this book. I got all of the information from this course better through the Security+ Dion training on Udemy. All of the security information overlaps with generalities and talk about malware and access control. Books become outdated quickly. I would recommend more recent and living information like Udemy courses or articles and X posts. AI is rapidly changing the landscape and a 2021 book isn't a good use of my time.

### *The Security Risk Assessment Handbook* (3rd ed.)
**Douglas J. Landoll** · 2021 · CRC Press

**What's in it:** how to run a security risk assessment from start to finish: scoping, data gathering, reviews of administrative, technical, and physical controls, risk analysis, and the final report.

**Thoughts:** A great walkthrough of an assessment. You can walk your papers through this as a framework and be done as fast as you can read the 500 pages of this document. That being said, you can find shorter, truncated, condensed or more direct versions of this same format online. 

### *Auditing IT Infrastructures for Compliance* (3rd ed.)
**Robert Johnson, Marty Weiss, Michael G. Solomon** · 2022 · Jones & Bartlett Learning

**What's in it:** U.S. compliance laws (FISMA, HIPAA, GLBA, SOX), audit scope, frameworks (COBIT, ISO 27001, NIST), planning and running an audit, and compliance in each IT domain. Includes cloud auditing.

**Thoughts:** As far as books go, this is good. I would probably find materials for a similar certificate and go through that material and earn a certificate such as ISC2 CGRC and create a mock audit instead.

### Primary sources
- [NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/53/r5/upd1/final): the control catalog. Free.
- [NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/30/r1/final): how to run a risk assessment. Free.
- [NICE Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/applied-cybersecurity/nice): cybersecurity work roles.

**Thoughts:** I'd definitely recommend going straight to the source for most up-to-date NIST standards. Plug these into an AI model and have a discussion about it, then find an online framework and create your own mock audits and risk assessments.

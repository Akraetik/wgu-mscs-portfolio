# D800 — Human-Computer Interaction

**Course 7 of 10** · May 30 – Jun 21, 2026 · 2 performance tasks · CITI human-subjects certification

**Time:** 23 calendar days · ~43 hours on WGU work · ~27 hours of outside courses *(from my study journal; see [Time per course](../README.md#time-per-course))*

**How it went:** My longest course. I was burned out after D797 and took a family trip, where I read the first five chapters of the textbook. The CITI certification took 5 hours. I spent about 10 hours learning Figma for Task 1, and Task 2 was one 10-hour night. Writing about WCAG and Nielsen's heuristics without having them memorized felt like imposter syndrome, so learning them cold became a goal.

I felt like HCI was the capstone and I spent a lot more time on this class than would have been required for a minimum pass. The CITI certificate is short and easy. The material can be quickly done and I learned Figma more for my own edification than the course itself. It does help and helps future creative pursuits but it was not required for this course.

## In one line

I evaluated a major social app's privacy settings, rebuilt the existing flow in Figma, and redesigned it. Reaching a privacy control went from six taps across four menu levels to one or two. I also peer-reviewed an accessibility proposal against WCAG 2.2.

## What the course covers

HCI principles from psychology, design, and engineering. It covers designing and evaluating user-centered systems with wireframing and eye tracking, the ethics of privacy, consent, and accessibility, basic analysis of user-testing data, and the CITI IRB human-subjects review.

## What I did

**Task 1: evaluate an existing system**
- Got my topic approved in a 30-minute meeting with my instructor: a widely used consumer app's mobile privacy and account settings.
- Named three problems: **poor discoverability** (controls spread over 30+ screens), **no granular, reversible control** over who sees old posts, and **no efficient bulk content management**.
- Set requirements as a baseline: authentication, audience control, retroactive visibility, content management, navigation, responsiveness.
- Chose the metrics: **task success rate** (primary) and **time-on-task** (secondary), with reasons for each.
- Rebuilt the existing interface screen by screen in **Figma** (a style guide, components, and the full flow), plus a low-fi version in draw.io. I used Figma for D800 and Visio for D799. Visio was required even though I didn't want to use it. So if you want to save time, just stick with Visio. Documented the layout, the UI component inventory, the user flows, and the pain points.
- Earned the **CITI Human Subjects Research (Social-Behavioral-Educational)** certificate.

**Task 2: improve it**
- Three redesigns, each tied to a Nielsen heuristic:
  1. **Granular, reversible bulk visibility.** Filter past content by date, audience, or type, then undo in one step. (*User control and freedom; flexibility and efficiency*)
  2. **A flat, searchable privacy dashboard.** Search jumps straight to a setting. (*Recognition rather than recall*)
  3. **One hub for content and privacy.** Two separate flows merged into one. (*Consistency and standards*)
- An updated wireframe: **6 taps / 4 levels → 1–2 taps** from a single entry point.
- A test plan with clear success thresholds: ≥50% less time on the bulk-correction task, ≥60% on finding a setting, ≥30% on compound tasks.
- A peer review of an accessibility proposal for veterans' connected prosthetics. I graded it against **WCAG 2.2** (2.5.8 target size, 4.5:1 and 3:1 contrast) and an error-rate metric.

## Skills used

`Figma (components, auto layout, styles, prototyping)` `draw.io` `wireframing` `usability metrics` `Nielsen's 10 heuristics` `information architecture` `WCAG 2.2` `IRB / informed consent` `UX writing`

## What I'd do differently

- **Run the test.** I wrote the plan but never ran it. Five people and a stopwatch would turn a proposal into data.
- **Ship it as a case study, not a paper.** Problem → evidence → redesign → before/after, on one scrolling page.
- **I spent about 10 extra hours learning Figma.** Worth it for my skills, but it helped stretch a 10-day course to three weeks. Know when you're learning for the job and when you're learning for the grade.

## Key ideas worth knowing

- Usable, useful, pleasurable, meaningful
- Wireframe → mockup → prototype (low-fi → high-fi)
- Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics; Fitts's law; Hick's law
- Task success rate, time-on-task, error rate, SUS (System Usability Scale)
- Eye tracking and heatmaps; think-aloud protocol
- SWOT analysis; personas; journey maps
- IRB, informed consent, the Belmont Report principles
- WCAG 2.2: perceivable, operable, understandable, robust (POUR)

## Books and reading

### *Interaction Design: Beyond Human-Computer Interaction* (6th ed.)
**Yvonne Rogers, Helen Sharp, Jennifer Preece** · 2023 · Wiley

**What's in it:** what interaction design is, cognitive and social aspects of use, emotional design, interfaces from GUIs to voice, AR, and wearables, data gathering and analysis, the design process, prototyping, and evaluation methods.

**Thoughts:** I read about half of this per course requirements. It is useful. But you need to effectively learn from books. For me, the online resources were superior and I drifted away from books for UX/HCI work.

### *Learn Human-Computer Interaction*
**Christopher Reid Becker** · 2020 · Packt

**What's in it:** HCI foundations, user research, personas, information architecture, wireframing and prototyping, usability testing, and designing for real people.

**Thoughts:** I skimmed this. 2020 is already 6 years old. There are better resources that are modern and kept up to date for me personally. Both books are useful and great if you prefer to read your knowledge. WGU gives you full access to these books during and I believe after the program so if you plan on staying in this field they are resources for some time to use if you want them. I did not find either book extremely helpful.

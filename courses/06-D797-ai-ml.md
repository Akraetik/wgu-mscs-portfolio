## In one line

I built an end-to-end ML pipeline on 37,000+ rows of public California hospital staffing data. It cleans the data, engineers features, clusters, classifies, and compares models. I also addressed bias, privacy, and data drift.

## What I did

- **Cleaned the data** (37,604 rows × 10 columns, 2009–2013):
  - Dropped 85 rows missing facility identifiers. You can't impute an ID.
  - Filled 187 missing ratio values with the **median**, not the mean, because the column is right-skewed.
  - Trimmed whitespace, standardized case, parsed dates, capped outliers.
- **Engineered features:**
  - A binary `High_Demand` target, defined relative to each role group (top quartile of hours *within* that role).
  - `Duration_Days` from the reporting period dates.
  - Label-encoded county, facility control type, and role.
- **Scaled** with Min-Max because the hours data is skewed and not Gaussian.
- **Clustered** with K-Means (k = 4, from the elbow method) and fed the cluster label to the classifier as a feature.
- **Classified** with a Random Forest (`class_weight='balanced'` for the 70/30 imbalance). Then I compared Logistic Regression, Gradient Boosting, and SVM.
- **Wrote up three ethics issues,** each with dataset and model mitigations:
  1. Bias against rural and under-represented facility types (stratified splits, SMOTE, SHAP explanations)
  2. Worker privacy if the data were ever linked to individuals (keep it aggregate, plus an acceptable-use policy)
  3. Temporal drift: the data stops in 2013 (show the date range on every output, retrain on a schedule)

## What I'd do differently — the big lesson

My Random Forest scored 94.8% accuracy. **A near-perfect score is a warning sign, not a win.** Months later I re-ran my original code and tested it. Here's what I found:

| Test (same data, same split) | Accuracy |
|---|---|
| Always guess "not high demand" (baseline) | 70.6% |
| My Random Forest, all features | 94.8% |
| A single decision tree using **only** hours + role | **99.96%** |
| Random Forest **without** the hours column | 81.7% |

- **Target leakage.** I defined "high demand" as the top 25% of hours *within each role*. Then I gave the model hours and role as inputs. A one-level-deep rule can rebuild that label almost perfectly, so the model wasn't predicting anything. Remove hours, and the real signal is 81.7% against a 70.6% baseline.
- **My clusters were sorting by county code.** I fed K-Means unscaled label codes (counties numbered 0–55) next to features scaled 0–1. The county number swamped everything. The four clusters are just counties 0–10, 11–24, 25–39, and 40–55, in alphabetical order. **Scale every feature, and never cluster on label-encoded IDs.**
- **Preprocessing before the split.** I fit the scaler, the clusters, and the percentile thresholds on the full dataset. Fit them on training data only, inside a scikit-learn `Pipeline`.
- **Label encoding implies order.** Fine for trees. Wrong for distance-based methods like K-Means, SVM, and Logistic Regression. Use one-hot encoding there.

**The honest version.** I reframed it as a real question: predict 2013's high-demand roles from 2012's data. Train on 2009–2012, test on 2013. The Random Forest scored 95.8%. A one-line rule, "same as last year," also scored 95.8%. The model added nothing over the simplest guess. **Always compare against a naive rule, not just a dummy baseline.**

Finding these flaws in my own work is the most useful thing this course taught me.

## Key ideas worth knowing

- Supervised vs. unsupervised learning; classification vs. regression vs. clustering
- Train/validation/test splits; cross-validation; data leakage
- Accuracy vs. precision vs. recall vs. F1; confusion matrix; baselines
- Feature engineering; one-hot vs. label encoding; scaling (Min-Max vs. standardization)
- Elbow method; inertia; silhouette score
- Ensembles: bagging (Random Forest) vs. boosting (GBM)
- Hyperparameter search (`GridSearchCV`, `RandomizedSearchCV`); MLflow for tracking
